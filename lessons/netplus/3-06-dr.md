# Disaster recovery

Disaster recovery (DR) is how an organization **gets IT services back** after a major disruption such as a fire, flood, ransomware attack, or long power outage. Network+ tests the **metrics** that set recovery goals (RPO, RTO, MTTR, MTBF), the **recovery site** options, **high-availability** designs, and how plans are **tested**.

## DR, business continuity and impact analysis

- **Disaster recovery plan (DRP):** documents how **IT systems** and data are **restored** after a disruption: who does what, in what order, using which backups and sites.
- **Business continuity plan (BCP):** broader. It covers keeping the **whole business** running during and after a disruption (people, facilities, manual workarounds, communications). The DRP is one part of business continuity.
- **Business impact analysis (BIA):** the analysis done **first**. It identifies **critical business functions**, what losing each one costs over time, and therefore the **recovery priorities and targets** (RTO and RPO) for each system.

**Why it matters:** not every system needs the same protection. Payroll might tolerate a day of downtime; online ordering might not tolerate ten minutes. The BIA puts numbers on that so money goes where it matters.

**High availability vs disaster recovery:** **HA** keeps a service running through **small failures** (a failed power supply, switch, or server) with redundancy and automatic failover. **DR** handles **large events** that take out a whole site or system. You usually need both.

## RPO and RTO

These two targets come out of the BIA and drive the whole design.

| Metric | Question it answers | Measured | Driven by |
|---|---|---|---|
| **RPO** (recovery point objective) | How much **data** can we afford to **lose**? | Time **backward** from the disaster to the last good copy | **Backup or replication frequency** |
| **RTO** (recovery time objective) | How long can we be **down**? | Time **forward** from the disaster until service is restored | **Recovery method and site type** |

**Worked examples:**
- Backups run every **6 hours**. If disaster strikes just before the next backup, up to **6 hours of data** is lost, so the achievable RPO is 6 hours.
- "We can lose **15 minutes** of data and must be back online within **1 hour**" → **RPO = 15 minutes, RTO = 1 hour**.
- A bank needs **near-zero data loss** for transactions → **synchronous (continuous) replication** to a second site. Nightly backups could never meet that RPO.
- A company needs service back in **minutes** → a **hot site** or active-active design. A cold site can't meet that RTO.

Lower RPO and RTO cost more: more frequent replication, more standby hardware, faster links.

## MTTR and MTBF

These measure **reliability and repair** of components and systems, rather than recovery targets.

| Metric | Meaning | Better when |
|---|---|---|
| **MTBF** (mean time between failures) | **Average operating time between failures** of a repairable component | **Higher**: it fails less often |
| **MTTR** (mean time to repair or recover) | **Average time to detect, diagnose, and fix** a failure and restore service | **Lower**: it's fixed faster |

**Example:** a switch has an MTBF of 10,000 hours and an MTTR of 2 hours. You want MTBF to go **up** and MTTR to go **down**.

**How to reduce MTTR:**
- Keep **spare parts on site** (or a support contract with fast, e.g. 4-hour, replacement).
- Have **up-to-date documentation** and **configuration backups** ready to load.
- Good **monitoring** so failures are detected immediately.

**How to raise MTBF:** buy quality hardware, keep it cool and on clean power (UPS), and replace components before end of life.

## Recovery sites

If the main site is lost, where do you run?

| Site | What's there | Recovery time | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Hot site** | Fully equipped, running systems with **current (replicated) data** | **Minutes** to hours | **Highest** |
| **Warm site** | Hardware and connectivity in place, but **data must be restored** and systems configured | **Hours to days** | Medium |
| **Cold site** | **Space, power, and cooling only**: no equipment, no data | **Days to weeks** | **Lowest** |

- **Choosing:** match the site to the **RTO**. An RTO of 30 minutes needs a hot site; an RTO of two weeks can use a cold site.
- **Cloud site / DRaaS (disaster recovery as a service):** a cloud provider hosts **replicated systems** that can be **started during a disaster**. This gives hot or warm capability without building a second data center, and you pay mostly for storage until you fail over.
- **Geographic diversity:** put the recovery site **far enough away** that one regional disaster (hurricane, earthquake, regional power failure) can't hit both sites.
- **ISP diversity:** use **different providers and physical paths** so one carrier outage or cable cut doesn't take the site offline.
- **Restoring network configs at a DR site:** if the hardware differs, **interface names and hardware-specific settings** may need adjusting before a backup config will work.

## High availability designs

**High availability (HA)** minimizes downtime through **redundancy** and **automatic failover**. The two classic layouts:

| | **Active-active** | **Active-passive** |
|---|---|---|
| How it works | **All** nodes (or sites) carry traffic **at the same time** and share the load | One node is **active**; a **standby** node waits and takes over if it fails |
| Benefit | Uses all capacity; often better performance | **Simpler**; guaranteed capacity on failover because the standby was idle |
| Drawback | More complex; capacity planning is critical | **Idle hardware** you paid for |

**The active-active capacity trap:** two firewalls each running at **70%** load share traffic. If one fails, the survivor would need **140%** of its capacity and becomes overloaded. In a two-node active-active pair, keep each node **under about 50%** so either one can carry the whole load alone.

**Failover** protocols you may see: **FHRP** (VRRP, HSRP) for gateways, firewall HA pairs, and server **clustering**.

## Redundancy and single points of failure

A **single point of failure (SPOF)** is any component whose failure **takes down the whole service**: one internet link, one core switch, one power feed, one DHCP server. HA design removes them.

- **N+1 redundancy:** **one more component than needed**. If a room needs 3 cooling units, install 4 so one can fail without losing capacity. (**2N** means a full duplicate of everything.)
- **Redundant power:** **dual, hot-swappable power supplies** that share the load, fed from **different circuits** and UPSs; generators for long outages.
- **Redundant links and devices:** two uplinks, two core switches, link aggregation, and **diverse paths** so both cables don't run through the same trench.
- **Clustering:** several servers **appear as one system** and **fail over automatically** if a member fails.
- **Load balancing:** spreads traffic across several servers and stops sending to one that fails health checks.

Scenario: "If the one router at the branch fails, the branch is offline" → that router is a SPOF; add a second router with an FHRP, or a backup cellular link.

## Backups and snapshots

**Backup types:**

| Type | What it copies | Backup speed | Restore needs | Archive bit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Full** | **Everything** | Slowest | Just the full | Cleared |
| **Incremental** | Changes since the **last backup of any type** | **Fastest** | The full **plus every incremental** since | Cleared |
| **Differential** | Changes since the **last full** | Grows each day | The full **plus only the latest differential** | **Not cleared** |

Example: full on Sunday, failure on Thursday. With incrementals you restore Sunday + Mon + Tue + Wed. With differentials you restore Sunday + Wednesday's differential.

**3-2-1 rule:** keep **3 copies** of data, on **2 different media** types, with **1 copy offsite**. Many add a copy that is **offline (air-gapped) or immutable**, because **ransomware** encrypts or deletes any backup it can reach.

**Snapshots:** a **point-in-time copy of a VM or volume** used for **quick rollback** (e.g. before a risky upgrade). Snapshots usually live on the **same storage**, so if that storage fails they're gone too. They are **not a replacement for backups**.

**Test restores:** a backup you have never restored is a hope, not a plan.

## Testing the plan

A DR plan that has never been tested will fail in surprising ways (missing passwords, wrong phone numbers, a backup nobody can restore). Network+ names **tabletop exercises** and **validation tests**.

| Test | What happens | Risk to production |
|---|---|---|
| **Tabletop exercise** | Key staff **talk through** a disaster scenario around a table, step by step. **No systems are touched** | None |
| **Walkthrough / checklist review** | Teams review the documented steps, contact lists, and responsibilities | None |
| **Validation tests** | **Actually perform recovery steps** to prove they work: restore a backup, bring up DR systems, check data | Low to medium |
| **Parallel test** | DR systems are brought up and **run alongside production**; users stay on production | Low |
| **Full interruption (failover) test** | Production is **actually shut down** and everything fails over to DR | **Highest**, but most realistic |

- **Test regularly:** at least **annually** and **after major changes** (new systems, new site, staff turnover).
- **After-action report:** after every test or real event, record **lessons learned** and **update the plan**.

## Common mix-ups

- **RPO vs RTO:** RP**O** = **data loss** (how far back you go); RT**O** = **downtime** (how long until you're back). Think "**P**oint in the past" vs "**T**ime to recover".
- **MTBF vs MTTR:** MTBF is time **between** failures (want it **high**); MTTR is time **to repair** (want it **low**).
- **RTO vs MTTR:** RTO is a **target** the business sets; MTTR is a **measured average** of actual repairs.
- **Warm vs cold site:** warm has **hardware** but needs data restored; cold has **only space, power, and cooling**.
- **Hot site vs active-active:** a hot site is a **standby location** ready to take over; active-active means both locations **carry traffic now**.
- **Incremental vs differential:** incremental = since **last backup of any kind** (many pieces to restore); differential = since **last full** (two pieces to restore).
- **Snapshot vs backup:** a snapshot is a quick rollback point, usually on the same storage; a backup is a separate copy.
- **Tabletop vs validation test:** tabletop **talks**; validation **does**.
- **BCP vs DRP:** BCP = keep the **business** running; DRP = restore **IT**.

## Key terms

- **BIA:** business impact analysis; identifies critical functions and sets RTO/RPO.
- **RPO:** maximum acceptable data loss, measured in time.
- **RTO:** maximum acceptable downtime.
- **MTBF:** average operating time between failures.
- **MTTR:** average time to repair or recover.
- **Hot / warm / cold site:** recovery sites from fully ready to empty space.
- **DRaaS:** cloud-hosted disaster recovery.
- **Active-active / active-passive:** HA designs that share load or keep a standby.
- **N+1:** one more component than required. **2N:** a full duplicate of every component.
- **SPOF:** a single point of failure.
- **3-2-1 rule:** 3 copies, 2 media, 1 offsite.
- **Tabletop exercise:** a discussion-based walk through a disaster scenario.
- **After-action report:** lessons learned recorded after a test or incident.

> **Exam tip:** RP**O** = how much data you can lose; RT**O** = how long you can be down. Lower MTTR and higher MTBF are better. "Fastest recovery, highest cost" → **hot site**; "just power and space" → **cold site**. "Talk through the plan, no systems touched" → **tabletop**. Two active-active nodes at 70% each will overload when one fails.
